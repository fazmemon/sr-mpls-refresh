Capacity arithmetic

The two bits of maths worth being able to do out loud — Clos fabric N−1, and backbone N−1. Both answer the same question: how full can I run this before a single failure hurts?

1 · Clos fabric — the spine ceiling

A leaf spreads its uplinks across S spines. Lose F of them and the survivors carry everything.

Two formulas, two different questions. Don't mix them.
S = number of spines. F = number of spines you must survive losing — a count, not a percentage. U = current utilisation.

Q1 — how full can I safely run?

Umax = (S − F) / S

Depends only on spine counts. Your current utilisation does not appear.

Q2 — where do I land after a failure?

Uafter = Ubefore × S / (S − F)

Above 100% means oversubscribed — demand exceeds surviving capacity.

Same load, same single failure, three different outcomes — purely from how wide the spine layer is. Cross-check against Q1: 80% is above the 75% ceiling for 4 spines (so it breaks) and below the 87.5% ceiling for 8 (so it doesn't).

The design lever: don't buy more redundancy on a narrow spine layer — buy a wider one. Redundancy gets cheaper as S grows, because each spine is a smaller fraction of the whole. Four fat spines makes every spine catastrophic; sixteen makes a spine loss a rounding error, for similar money.

Worked example — scenario 4, the long way

4 spines, running at 80%, spine 3 dies. Thinking in "spine-units" is the version to do out loud, because it needs no formula:

  • Each spine = 1 unit of capacity. Four spines = 4 units.
  • Running at 80% ⇒ demand = 0.80 × 4 = 3.2 units.
  • Lose one spine ⇒ capacity = 3.0 units.
  • 3.2 into 3.0 = 107%. Oversubscribed by 7%, with nowhere for it to go.

Same answer as Uafter = 0.80 × 4/3. And Q1 already warned you: the ceiling for 4 spines is 75%, so 80% was over the line before anything failed.

Why 7% short costs far more than 7%: all-reduce is paced by its slowest participant. Every GPU waits for the last one. So a 7% capacity shortfall on one path stalls the whole job, not 7% of it. Degradation in collective workloads is non-linear — that's the sentence to say out loud.

Related: PFC firing means ECN/DCQCN didn't back senders off in time. PFC is a backstop, not a mechanism. And rail-optimised placement reduces spine dependence in the first place — intra-rail collectives never touch a spine.

2 · Backbone / WAN — N−1 worst-case link utilisation

A mesh has no single ratio, because where the traffic lands after a failure depends on the topology. The standard method:

For every single failure (each link, each node), recompute routing and record the highest resulting utilisation on any link. The worst of those numbers is your N−1 max link utilisation. Size so it stays under your target.

That's one number for the whole network, it's computable from the topology and a traffic matrix, and it's what capacity planning tools actually produce. Quoting it beats quoting "N+1".

The special cases worth knowing cold

  • Ring of any size: a single cut sends traffic the long way, so the surviving path carries its own load plus the failed path's. Steady-state ceiling is 50%.
  • Two parallel paths: same thing — 50%.
  • k parallel paths: (k−1)/k, same form as the Clos ceiling.
  • Partial mesh: no shortcut. Compute it.
Protection is not capacity. TI-LFA guarantees a loop-free path exists within 50 ms. It says nothing about whether that path has room. A fabric can be 100% protected and still congest the instant it repairs. Two separate purchases, two separate arguments — people conflate them constantly.
Diversity is not capacity either. A second path stops you being cut off. It doesn't stop you being congested. Same conflation, different flavour.

Size to repair time, not to a formula

The exposure to a second failure is probability × duration. A 4-hour terrestrial repair and an 11-week subsea repair are the same failure with ~450× the exposure window.

And a long repair makes the problem cheaper, not dearer. Eleven weeks is long enough to procure. So you don't buy standing capacity for the second failure — you buy the option: a burstable contract, a pre-negotiated wavelength with a committed turn-up SLA, an IRU. Fast failures need pre-built capacity; slow ones can be answered commercially. Capital cost becomes contingent cost.

And an 11-week degraded mode is not an incident, it's the operating state for a quarter — so it gets designed: standing QoS policy, scheduling changes, and for research workloads, move the compute to the data rather than the data to the compute.

3 · Elephant flows break all of this

Every number above assumes traffic divides. It doesn't, when there are only a few enormous flows.

  • One flow cannot be spread by per-flow ECMP. One hash, one link, by design. A single transfer on a 4×10G bundle gets 10G, and no amount of capacity changes that.
  • Fixes are parallel streams, adaptive routing / flowlet switching, or packet spraying with the receiver handling reorder.
  • Incast is a ratio, not a rate. Ten senders at 10G onto one port is 10:1; upgrading that port to 25G leaves 4:1. Faster links shorten the overrun, they don't remove it. What decides it is burst size in bytes versus buffer depth — a silicon question, not a link-speed one.
  • And a 25G port can be allocated a shallower share of a shared buffer pool than the 10G port it replaced. You can upgrade the link and make the drops worse.

4 · Averages lie

  • 40% over five minutes can contain 100% for 200 µs. A 10G egress buffer fills in microseconds.
  • "Zero drops" may be the wrong counter — interface drops often exclude output queue discards.
  • The proof is buffer occupancy, not utilisation: most ASICs expose a max queue-depth watermark since last read. A watermark near the limit on a port averaging 40% is unambiguous.
  • Poll sub-second via streaming telemetry, not 5-minute SNMP.

5 · One-line versions

Clos ceiling: "With four spines, 75% is the N−1 line."  (S−F)/S

Clos after a failure: "We were at 80%, so losing one of four put us at 107%."  U × S/(S−F)

Width: "Same 80% on eight spines lands at 91%, on sixteen at 85%. Wider spine layer, cheaper redundancy."

Backbone: "I'd size to N−1 worst-case link utilisation across every single failure, not to a redundancy formula."

Ring: "A ring is 50%. A cut sends traffic the long way and the survivor carries both loads."

Protection: "TI-LFA guarantees a path exists in 50 ms. It doesn't guarantee the path has room."

Repair time: "Exposure is probability times duration, and eleven weeks is long enough to buy your way out — so I'd hold an option, not idle capacity."

Elephants: "One flow is one hash is one link. Capacity doesn't fix that; parallelism or spraying does."
